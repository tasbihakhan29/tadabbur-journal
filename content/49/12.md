This is one of those verses that becomes harder to read the more honestly you read it.

It begins with suspicion — assuming the worst about people before there is any evidence. I noticed Allah did not say "avoid suspicion" outright; He said "avoid much suspicion, for some suspicion is sin." There is room for caution and discernment in life, but the verse draws a line at the suspicion that hardens into accusation without proof, the kind that colors how we see someone before they have done anything to deserve it.

Then comes spying — actively searching for what people have not chosen to show you. And then backbiting, mentioned in the same breath, as if the verse is tracing a single path: suspicion leads to surveillance, and surveillance leads to talk.

The image Allah uses for backbiting is the one I cannot get out of my head. Would you eat the flesh of your dead brother? Every translation I read tried to soften it, and every one of them still landed with the same force. It is visceral on purpose. We are quick to speak about people behind their backs in ways we would never dare to their faces, and the Qur'an refuses to let that feel small.

What humbled me most is realizing how easily backbiting hides inside ordinary conversation. It rarely announces itself as sin. It shows up as "venting," as "just being honest," as concern phrased as gossip. I had to ask myself how many conversations I have been part of where someone's absence was the only reason the conversation was possible at all.

The verse ends, as so many of these verses do, with mercy rather than condemnation: "Allah is the Accepter of Repentance, Most Merciful." Even after naming something this serious, the door back is left open. That, to me, is the difference between a verse that wants to shame and a verse that wants to reform.

~ Tasbiha Khan
